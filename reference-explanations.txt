purpose-claims|A contingent claim pays according to what happens in a future situation.|Think of a fire policy: it pays if a covered fire occurs. A share depends on the business’s results. To understand the contract, ask who pays whom, after which event, and how much money changes hands.
purpose-sharing|A farmer may prefer a more dependable income even when the average dollar amount is lower.|Losing money needed to keep the farm running can be more damaging than an equal extra gain is helpful. Insurance transfers a specified risk for a premium, the price of coverage. Pooling helps when different farmers do not all lose together.
purpose-liability|An ordinary shareholder generally risks the money invested, without having to pay company debts from unrelated personal assets.|The shares can still become worthless. Limited liability limits responsibility for additional company losses; it does not guarantee repayment of the investment. Personal guarantees and legal exceptions must be considered separately.
purpose-adverse|People who privately know they are more likely to claim may be more eager to buy insurance.|The insurer may therefore attract a riskier group than it expects. This information problem happens before the agreement. Checking risk and encouraging broad participation can help, but those choices also affect cost and access.
purpose-moral|The owner takes less care after obtaining protection. That changed incentive is called moral hazard.|The insurer pays part of a covered loss, so prevention may feel less worthwhile to the owner. A deductible makes the customer pay part of the loss and can preserve a reason to be careful. Deliberate wrongdoing is not necessary.
purpose-agency|A manager may choose risks that improve this year’s bonus while leaving later losses to the owners.|One person makes decisions for another but is rewarded on a different timetable. That is a principal–agent problem. Monitoring and pay arrangements can improve alignment, although no contract removes every conflict.
purpose-innovation|A useful financial innovation helps people fund worthwhile activity or manage risk after its costs and weaknesses are considered.|More complicated contracts are not automatically better. Ask what problem the new arrangement solves, who takes the risk, what it costs, and what happens when conditions become difficult.
purpose-externality|A forced sale can lower prices for other holders and lead to further losses and selling.|The seller may only be trying to raise cash. Other institutions then face lower prices on the same assets. This wider effect is an externality because the original seller does not bear all the costs imposed on others.
risk-expected|Expected return is an average of possible percentage returns, weighted by their probabilities. It is not guaranteed.|State the period first, such as one year. Multiply each possible return by its chance and add the results. A positive average can coexist with a substantial chance of losing money in the actual year.
risk-variance|Variance and volatility describe how widely returns vary around their average.|Variance averages squared differences, so its units are squared return units. Its square root is standard deviation, also called volatility, measured in return units for a stated period. It counts unusually good outcomes as deviations too, and does not describe every extreme loss.
risk-correlation|Holdings can offset some of each other’s fluctuations when their returns do not move perfectly together.|Correlation is a unitless number from −1 to +1, not a return percentage. Portfolio risk also depends on the amount invested in each holding and each holding’s volatility. Correlations can change, especially during stress. Zero correlation alone does not establish full statistical independence.
risk-systematic|Owning many shares can still leave exposure to common industry or economic problems.|A failed product may mainly hurt one company. A recession can hurt many companies together. Diversification reduces dependence on individual companies, but it does not make the portfolio independent of the economy.
risk-beta|Beta measures how returns move with a selected market benchmark; it does not measure every risk.|Beta is unitless. Its estimate depends on the benchmark and measurement period. A low-beta business can still fail for company-specific reasons, or suffer an unusual large loss that a simple market relationship misses.
risk-capm|Basic CAPM links required return to shared market exposure, measured by beta.|CAPM means Capital Asset Pricing Model. It adds beta times the expected market risk premium to a risk-free rate. The rates must refer to the same period, usually percent per year. Real returns can depend on risks and patterns that this simple model omits.
risk-frontier|On the modeled efficient frontier, a better expected return requires more volatility, given the available choices.|Both measures must use a stated time period. The frontier is calculated from estimates and investment restrictions. If those estimates change, the supposedly best portfolio can change too. The model does not promise which holding will perform best in real life.
risk-cml|The capital market line uses total volatility. The security market line uses beta.|The capital market line concerns efficient portfolios mixing a risk-free asset with a particular risky portfolio. The security market line is CAPM’s expected-return comparison across beta. Beta is unitless; volatility and expected return use stated return periods.
risk-lln|Pooling many sufficiently independent losses can make the average loss easier to predict.|This is the law of large numbers. If one event damages many insured properties together, the losses do not average away. Reinsurance transfers some insurer risk to another insurer; reserves provide resources for claims. Neither prevents the original damage.
risk-var|A 99% daily Value at Risk leaves a modeled 1% chance of exceeding the stated daily loss threshold.|The exceedance probability is 100% − 99% = 1%. It does not tell you how large those worse losses can be. The threshold is a model output in loss units, such as dollars; it cannot be calculated from the confidence percentage alone.
risk-tails|Rare extreme outcomes can be more common, and investments can move together more strongly, than a simple model assumes.|Fat tails describes the shape of possible outcomes, not a fixed loss percentage. A short data sample may contain no example of an important rare event. Stress tests add severe scenarios, but do not guarantee that all future problems have been covered.
risk-utility|A loss threatening basic needs can hurt more than an equal extra gain would help.|Utility is a model of how valuable an outcome is to a person. Diminishing marginal utility means another dollar often matters less as wealth rises. Expected utility and behavioral loss aversion are related ways to discuss choices, but they are different theories.
behavior-news|Good results can disappoint if investors expected even better results or a better outlook.|Share prices reflect beliefs about future payments. The relevant news is the change from those beliefs. A positive earnings headline therefore does not guarantee a price rise.
behavior-emh|Semi-strong efficiency concerns information that is publicly available.|Weak-form efficiency concerns past prices and trading data. Strong-form efficiency would include private information too, which is not a realistic general description. Efficiency claims need to specify which information investors could already use.
behavior-walk|Unpredictable news does not require expected returns to stay constant forever.|Investors may require different compensation for risk in different conditions. A strict random-walk model makes stronger assumptions than many efficiency models. Predictable returns associated with changing risk do not automatically create a riskless trading profit.
behavior-joint|To identify an unusually high return, researchers must first choose a model of normal compensation for risk.|A result may look exceptional because the market price is wrong, or because the comparison model leaves out a risk. This difficulty is called the joint-hypothesis problem. One test alone does not automatically distinguish the explanations.
behavior-excess|Shiller challenged simple valuation models that use a constant rate to translate future dividends into today’s price.|Share prices fluctuated more than those simple accounts could readily explain. Changing required returns and risk compensation can matter too. The result questions the model; it does not prove that every large price move is irrational.
behavior-prospect|People often judge outcomes as gains or losses from a reference point, and losses can weigh more heavily.|Prospect theory also describes diminishing sensitivity and uneven responses to probabilities. These patterns help explain choices that a simple average-dollar calculation misses. Loss aversion is different from merely preferring less uncertainty.
behavior-anchoring|The purchase price can become a mental target even after the investment’s prospects change.|This is anchoring. From today onward, the decision is between holding and using the money elsewhere. Relevant taxes can depend on the purchase price, but the old price does not force a future recovery.
behavior-disposition|The disposition effect is a tendency to sell winners quickly while keeping losing investments too long.|Keeping a holding avoids the act of realizing a loss, but not the economic loss already reflected in its value. Ask whether you would buy it today, given the same information and alternatives.
behavior-confidence|Overconfidence can lead to too much trading, too much money in one position, and too little allowance for being wrong.|A persuasive explanation is not evidence that a forecast is accurate. Trading costs and relevant taxes reduce the money that remains. Even a useful prediction must be strong enough to cover the costs of acting on it.
behavior-bubbles|Rising prices can attract buyers, reinforce optimistic stories and support more borrowing.|Those purchases can push prices up again, creating feedback. Real business prospects may also improve, so a popular story alone does not establish a bubble. The question is whether the price can be justified by plausible future benefits and risks.
behavior-arbitrage|Trading against an apparently excessive price can fail because timing, borrowing costs and funding needs matter.|A short seller may face rising losses before a later decline. The lender of the shares may request their return, and the trader may need extra collateral. An eventual valuation correction does not guarantee the trader can stay in the position until then.
behavior-narratives|Stories shared across society can affect spending, investment and willingness to take risks.|A narrative can influence what people expect and what they do together. It is one mechanism behind economic feedback. A compelling story still needs to be checked against evidence and the actual financial claim.
bonds-face|The annual coupon payment (C) is $50; the face value (F), or principal repaid at maturity, is $1,000.|These are dollar amounts. The assumed coupon rate is 5% per year. Convert it to 0.05 and multiply: $1,000 × 0.05 = $50 interest each year. At maturity, the last $50 coupon plus $1,000 principal gives $1,050. The purchase price can differ from face value.
bonds-time|The payment-period index (t) runs through 1, 2 and 3; the total number of payment periods until maturity (T) stays fixed at 3.|The example lasts three years and pays once per year. The index labels which payment is being valued. If the same term paid twice per year instead, 3 years × 2 payments per year would give 6 payment periods. Coupon amounts and yield must then use half-year periods too.
bonds-market|Investors and issuers agree on offered terms, while trades between investors establish prices that imply yields.|The issuer can propose a coupon and issue price, but cannot force a purchase. Underwriters gather investor demand, called bookbuilding. A quoted comparable market yield is an annual percentage observed from similar bonds’ prices and promises, rather than a universal rate imposed on every bond.
bonds-ytminverse|Yield to maturity is the annual percentage that connects a bond’s current price with all its promised future payments.|The price and payments are dollars; the yield is a rate with a stated compounding convention. With a market price, calculate the rate. In a valuation exercise, choose a required rate and calculate the price instead. Bonds permitting early repayment may need other yield measures as well.
bonds-current|Current yield measures annual coupon income relative to purchase price. Yield to maturity also includes principal repayment and payment timing.|Current yield = annual coupon dollars ÷ purchase-price dollars × 100. Its result is an annual income percentage. Buying below face value can add a later gain that current yield leaves out. Neither calculation automatically subtracts losses from possible default.
bonds-reinvest|To turn YTM into the same terminal compound growth rate, received coupons must be reinvested at that rate.|A coupon is money paid before final maturity. What that money earns afterward affects your wealth at the end. YTM remains the internal rate of the promised cash-flow schedule if payments arrive, but reinvestment is a separate decision. A zero-coupon bond has no interim coupon payments to reinvest.
bonds-price|The fixed-payment bond’s dollar price falls when the required yield rises, with other relevant terms unchanged.|The contract still promises the same future dollars. A buyer requiring a larger annual percentage return must pay less for those dollars today. This is why purchase price can change while the contractual coupon rate stays the same.
bonds-spot|A different future payment date can have a different spot rate; a bond’s YTM summarizes several payment dates in one rate.|A spot rate applies to money at a particular maturity. Consistent pricing can value each payment using the rate for its date, then calculate the single YTM implied by the resulting price. Compare matching currencies and rate conventions.
bonds-forward|An implied forward rate is a pricing relationship for a named future period, not a guaranteed forecast.|It connects alternative ways to invest across successive periods at today’s prices. The rate is an annual percentage under the example’s convention. Actual future rates can differ because conditions change and today’s prices can include compensation for uncertainty.
bonds-duration|The example gives approximately an 8% price decline for a one-percentage-point yield increase.|The assumed modified duration is 8. A one-percentage-point change is 1 ÷ 100 = 0.01 in decimal yield. The estimate is −8 × 0.01 = −0.08, or −8% of the starting price. This is a local approximation; curvature and changing cash flows can affect the actual result.
bonds-convexity|The relationship between bond price and yield is curved, and early-repayment rights can change the payments investors receive.|Convexity describes that curvature. A normal fixed-payment bond without embedded options has positive convexity. A callable bond or mortgage investment can behave differently: when rates fall, a borrower may repay early, limiting the investor’s benefit from further rate declines.
bonds-curve|An inverted yield curve is a warning indicator, not a certain recession prediction.|The curve compares annual yields with different repayment dates. It is inverted when shorter-term yields exceed longer-term yields. Expected policy changes, compensation for holding longer debt and investor demand all affect its shape. The signal does not specify a guaranteed event date.
bonds-credit|A credit spread includes more than the expected loss from default.|It is a difference between comparable annual yields, measured in percentage points. Investors can also require compensation for uncertainty or difficulty selling. A high promised yield may therefore coexist with a modest expected return after considering missed payments and recovery.
bonds-clean|The actual settlement payment usually adds accrued interest to the clean quoted price.|Accrued interest is interest accumulated since the last coupon date. Clean price and accrued interest are dollar amounts; adding them gives the dirty price paid to complete the trade. The dates and the contract’s day-count convention determine the interest amount.
bonds-real|Inflation reduces what an investment’s money return can buy.|Nominal return describes growth in money. Real return adjusts for price growth over the same period. Use nominal rates with nominal cash flows, and real rates with purchasing-power-adjusted cash flows. Inflation-linked bonds follow their specified index, which may differ from one household’s expenses.
bonds-annuity|An annuity pays over a defined schedule; a perpetuity is an idealized stream continuing indefinitely.|The exercises use equal dollar payments at each year-end and a positive constant annual discount rate. A lifetime annuity instead depends on how long the covered person lives. A consol is a perpetual bond. Payment amounts, dates and rates must be matched before applying a formula.
equity-residual|Creditors and other higher-priority claims come before ordinary shareholders when a company is liquidated.|Liquidation means closing the company and distributing what can be recovered from its assets. Common shareholders own the remainder, called residual value. They may receive nothing. Limited liability protects against some additional obligations but does not prevent losing the whole investment.
equity-preferred|Preferred shares usually have specified payment priority over common shares, with potentially different voting rights.|Check the agreement. Dividends may be suspended, and some unpaid preferred dividends accumulate for later consideration. Preferred shares are still an equity claim under their terms, not automatically a bond with certain interest payments.
equity-gordon|The Gordon model assumes dividends grow forever at a constant annual percentage below the required annual return.|D₁ is next year’s dividend in dollars per share. Required return and growth are annual rates entered as decimals. The result is dollars per share. If growth reaches or exceeds the required rate, the simple infinite-payment formula no longer gives the intended finite valuation.
equity-wacc|Valuing cash available to all capital providers gives a business or enterprise value before the adjustments needed for ordinary equity.|Capital providers include lenders and shareholders. Subtract relevant debt and higher-priority claims, and handle nonoperating cash consistently to estimate equity value. Cash flows belonging only to equity use the required equity return instead. Both approaches need consistent treatment to avoid counting the same value twice.
equity-terminal|A large share of a valuation can depend on future years that are not forecast individually.|Terminal value estimates those later cash flows at a future date, then is discounted to today. Small changes in assumed long-run growth or required return can have large dollar effects. Show sensitivity to plausible assumptions instead of treating one precise number as certain.
equity-pe|A low price-to-earnings ratio can reflect low growth, risk or unusual earnings, as well as a low price.|P/E divides dollars per share by earnings dollars per share for a stated earnings period. It is usually described as times earnings, not percent per year. Compare consistent accounting periods. Negative earnings or a temporarily large profit can make the ratio misleading.
equity-cape|CAPE uses a ten-year average of inflation-adjusted earnings to reduce reliance on one unusually good or bad year.|The ten years are part of its definition. CAPE is a valuation ratio, not an annual return percentage. It can help frame long-horizon expectations, but does not reliably time next month’s market. Accounting, industry composition and interest rates complicate comparisons across decades.
equity-marketcap|Market capitalization is share price multiplied by the number of shares outstanding.|Dollars per share × shares gives dollars of equity value. It is different from annual revenue or enterprise value. A price rise can increase market capitalization without any new money being paid into the company.
equity-dilution|A lower ownership percentage does not necessarily mean a lower dollar value for an existing owner.|New shares bring new money or assets into the company. Whether that compensates old owners depends on the issue price and how the resources are used. Distinguish a smaller fraction of a larger business from an actual loss of economic value.
equity-buyback|A buyback distributes company cash and reduces shares outstanding; it does not automatically create business value.|Earnings per share can rise just because the share count falls. Ask what price the company paid, what borrowing it used and what other uses the cash had. A dividend also transfers value out of the business, so the share price tends to adjust for the payout with other things equal.
equity-npv|A project has positive net present value when the present dollar value of its additional future cash exceeds its initial cost.|Discount each future cash flow using a rate appropriate to its risk and period. Include new costs and benefits caused by the decision, including relevant taxes and money tied up in operations. Exclude sunk costs that today’s decision cannot change. Accounting profit alone does not answer this question.
equity-capitalstructure|Borrowing changes who bears losses and can affect taxes, financial distress and management incentives.|Capital structure is the business’s mix of debt, equity and other funding. Idealized theories show circumstances where that mix would not change total value. Real businesses face costs and restrictions outside those assumptions, so the funding choice can matter.
housing-caprate|Cap rate describes annual operating income relative to property value, before financing and property-price changes.|Annual net operating income dollars ÷ property-value dollars × 100 gives the cap rate percentage. Net operating income subtracts operating expenses but usually excludes loan payments and income taxes. Check treatment of major capital spending, vacancies and reserves. It is not the owner’s complete return.
housing-rentbuy|Ownership costs include more than the mortgage payment: maintenance, taxes, insurance, transaction costs and tied-up capital matter.|Principal repayments reduce debt and build the owner’s stake, but still use cash. Compare the housing service received, how long you expect to stay and the flexibility needed. A resale gain is uncertain. Money used for a down payment also has alternative uses.
housing-amortization|As the loan balance falls, less interest is due, leaving more of a level monthly payment to repay principal.|Interest is charged on the remaining principal in dollars, using the monthly rate. A fixed total principal-and-interest payment can therefore contain changing portions over time. Taxes and insurance may be separate. At zero interest, divide principal by the number of payments.
housing-ltv|A high loan-to-value ratio leaves a small owner’s cushion that a modest property-price decline can erase.|LTV = outstanding loan dollars ÷ property-value dollars × 100. It is a percentage at a date, not an annual rate. Owner equity is property value minus debt before selling costs. Negative equity can make refinancing difficult; legal responsibility and incentives vary across loans and markets.
housing-refinance|The borrower can benefit from replacing a fixed-rate loan after market rates fall; the lender may then have to reinvest at lower rates.|Refinancing is taking a new loan to replace the old one. Compare fees and terms as well as the rate. Early mortgage repayment changes investors’ payment timing. If borrowers instead repay more slowly than expected, investors can remain exposed longer; that is extension risk.
housing-arm|An adjustable-rate mortgage can have higher future payments when its reset reference rate rises.|The contract specifies reset dates, the reference rate, any added margin and limits on changes. A low introductory annual percentage does not describe all later payments. A fixed contractual rate shifts more of the changing-rate exposure toward the lender.
housing-mbs|Mortgage-backed security investors receive money derived from a pool of home-loan payments.|The structure and servicing rules decide how payments reach investors. Defaults, guarantees and early repayments affect the result. A guarantee against missed borrower payments does not guarantee a stable market price or remove the effect of early principal repayment.
housing-tranches|Large combined mortgage losses can exhaust the lower-priority layers that protect senior investors.|A tranche is a layer with a stated payment priority. Lower layers absorb losses first. If many borrowers default together, total losses can exceed the cushion that looked adequate when defaults were assumed to be less connected.
housing-cds|A credit default swap transfers specified credit-event exposure through premium and protection payments.|The contract defines the event, the reference obligation and the settlement. A protection buyer still depends on the seller’s ability to pay. Differences between the protected exposure and the actual holding create mismatch risk. The contract does not duplicate every feature of owning the bond or ordinary insurance.
housing-reits|A REIT share is an ownership claim on a real-estate-related business, rather than direct ownership of a particular home.|REIT means real estate investment trust. The share can be easier to trade than a building, while still carrying property, borrowing, management and share-market risks. Tax and distribution requirements depend on the jurisdiction and structure.
derivatives-forward|For one unit at delivery, the long forward’s payoff is the asset’s settlement price minus the agreed delivery price.|Both prices must use the same units, such as dollars per share. Long means taking the buyer’s side. A newly agreed fair forward often has about zero initial value, but its delivery price is a different number. Unlike an option buyer, the forward buyer is obligated to perform.
derivatives-futures|Futures are standardized contracts whose gains and losses are usually settled daily through a clearing system.|This daily adjustment is marking to market. It can create a need for cash before the eventual economic benefit of the hedge arrives. Private forwards can also require collateral. Clearing manages counterparty exposure but does not remove funding needs or make the clearing system itself risk-free.
derivatives-carry|The simple forward-price formula applies to an asset with no income and no other holding costs or benefits, under its financing assumptions.|Its output is dollars per unit of the asset. The rate is a continuously compounded annual rate and time is years. Income such as dividends reduces the relevant carry cost; storage can increase it. Commodity ownership can also have a practical availability benefit. Currency forwards involve both currencies’ interest rates.
derivatives-basis|The investment used as a hedge may not move exactly like the risk being protected.|A workshop might use a futures contract for a related material, delivery date or location. The hedge can reduce uncertainty without offsetting it perfectly. The remaining mismatch is basis risk.
derivatives-call|A positive call payoff can still leave the buyer with a loss after the premium is subtracted.|At expiration, compare stock dollars per share with the strike dollars per share. The payoff is the positive difference, or zero. Net profit subtracts the premium in dollars per share. If the call expires worthless, the buyer loses the premium, before other costs.
derivatives-put|A put can be bought without already owning the shares it gives the right to sell.|The option itself can often be sold to realize its value. Exercising a physically settled put can create other positions or require shares and funding. Merely owning the put does not automatically make the holder a short seller. Price and payoff examples must specify dollars per share.
derivatives-exercise|American-style exercise allows the holder to exercise before expiration under the contract’s terms.|European-style exercise happens at expiration. The names concern rights, not trading location. Exercise and selling the option are different actions. Dividends, interest and remaining time value can affect which choice is worthwhile.
derivatives-parity|Put–call parity follows from matching the final payments of two portfolios under the stated assumptions.|For matching European options on a stock with no dividends, a call plus enough cash for the strike matches a stock plus a put at expiration. All prices use compatible per-share units. Dividends, early exercise, funding costs and trading restrictions can change the relationship that applies.
derivatives-binomial|A stock-and-cash portfolio can match the option’s payoffs in both modeled states.|Divide the option-payoff difference by the stock-price difference to obtain shares per option on one share. Borrowing or lending supplies the remaining cash part. The model’s risk-free growth factor must lie between the down and up stock multipliers to avoid inconsistent prices.
derivatives-riskneutral|Risk-neutral probabilities are pricing weights, not necessarily forecasts of what will really happen.|They are unitless numbers consistent with the model’s traded prices and risk-free financing. In the formula, r is the rate for the modeled period, while u and d are stock-price multipliers. Real-world expected returns may include compensation for risk that these pricing weights treat differently.
derivatives-bs|A single constant-volatility assumption does not match all actual option prices across strikes and expirations.|The Black–Scholes–Merton model assumes a particular continuous price process and idealized trading. Implied volatility is the input that fits an observed price, usually quoted as an annualized percentage. Different fitted inputs reveal limits of the constant-volatility model; they do not promise future price variation.
derivatives-vol|Implied volatility is the model input that reproduces the option’s observed dollar price.|It is commonly quoted as an annualized percentage. It can reflect demand for protection and compensation for risk as well as expectations. Realized volatility measures variation that actually occurred over a selected period, so it need not equal implied volatility.
derivatives-greeks|The Greeks describe how an option price responds to changes in price, time, volatility and interest rates.|Delta is local sensitivity to the underlying price; gamma describes changes in delta. Theta concerns passing time, vega volatility, and rho interest rates. Always check units: theta is often dollars per share per day, while vega and rho are often quoted per one-percentage-point change rather than per decimal unit.
derivatives-deltahedge|Delta hedging aims to offset the option’s response to a small change in the underlying price.|For an option on one share, delta can be read as shares needed for a local hedge. It changes as conditions change, so the hedge needs adjustment. Sudden jumps, changing volatility, trading costs and funding needs remain. This is risk reduction, not a guarantee of profit.
derivatives-short|A short share position can have unlimited losses as the share price rises. A bought put generally risks the premium paid.|A short seller also faces share-borrowing fees, demands for collateral and possible requests to return borrowed shares. A put has an expiration date. A decline occurring afterward may not help its buyer, even if the long-run price view was correct.
derivatives-embedded|Option-like rights appear inside loans, guarantees and business decisions as well as standalone options.|A borrower may have the right to refinance; a company may choose to delay or abandon a project. Those choices change which future payments occur. Recognizing the right helps explain who benefits when conditions change and who bears the other side of that choice.
banking-balance|Customer deposits are liabilities to the bank: money it owes those customers.|The same deposit is an asset to its customer. Loans, securities and cash are bank assets. Equity is asset dollars minus liability dollars. A withdrawal normally reduces both the bank’s deposit liability and the assets used to pay it.
banking-capital|Bank capital is qualifying funding that can absorb losses, especially owners’ equity. It is not simply a cash reserve.|Capital is measured in dollars and in ratios to specified exposures. Cash and central-bank reserves sit on the asset side and help meet payments. A bank can have a loss cushion without enough immediate cash, or plenty of cash alongside insufficient asset value to cover what it owes.
banking-liquid|A bank may own valuable assets but be unable to turn them into cash quickly enough to meet withdrawals.|Liquidity concerns payment timing. Solvency concerns whether assets are worth enough to cover obligations. A hurried sale can realize a lower price than a patient sale, using up equity and turning a cash-timing problem into a loss problem too.
banking-creation|The bank records a loan asset and a matching deposit liability when it credits the borrower’s account.|The borrower owes the bank, and the bank owes the customer the deposit. Deposits expand without automatically increasing bank equity. Lending still needs capital, funding, liquidity and credit-risk management. Repayment of loan principal generally reverses the corresponding deposit creation.
banking-reserves|Banks face capital, funding, risk and borrower-demand constraints as well as any reserve requirements.|Reserves are central-bank balances used by banks to settle payments. Households use bank deposits instead. Adding reserves does not mechanically make a bank approve more loans; a bank still needs willing creditworthy borrowers and an economically viable lending decision.
banking-policy|Policy rates influence borrowing costs, asset prices, currencies and expectations, which can change spending and investment.|The rate is a percentage per year, but its effect on a borrower depends on loan terms and refinancing dates. The effects take time and differ across financial systems. A central bank influences conditions without directly choosing every traded bond’s yield.
banking-qe|QE is central-bank buying of financial assets, rather than a direct grant of cash to households.|QE means quantitative easing. The central bank adds securities to its assets, and reserve balances generally rise. Its effects depend on sellers’ subsequent choices and financing conditions. Reserve growth alone does not imply a proportionate increase in household spending.
banking-qt|A central bank can reduce its asset holdings by not replacing maturing securities or by selling assets.|This is quantitative tightening, or QT. Holdings and reserve balances are dollar amounts. The effects depend on implementation and demand for liquidity. Reducing the balance sheet and raising the policy rate are distinct actions, although either can tighten financial conditions.
banking-lender|Emergency central-bank lending can supply liquidity when ordinary funding breaks down, under the facility’s conditions.|The bank must meet relevant rules on eligibility, collateral and price. A loan provides cash but also creates an obligation to repay. It does not cancel losses larger than the bank’s equity. Repairing those losses requires additional capital or choices about who bears them.
banking-rwa|Risk-weighted assets combine exposures after regulatory weights have been applied, instead of just adding their dollar amounts.|A capital ratio compares qualifying capital dollars with RWA dollars. A leverage ratio provides a differently defined, broader exposure comparison. Capital quality, additional buffers and exposures not shown as ordinary balance-sheet assets can matter. The exercise’s 8% is only an illustrative assumption.
banking-liquidityrules|Liquidity rules examine both cash needs under short-term stress and whether longer-lived assets have suitable funding.|The liquidity coverage ratio compares high-quality liquid assets with modeled stressed outflows. The net stable funding ratio examines the stability of funding relative to assets. These timing checks complement capital rules, which concern absorbing losses.
banking-insurance|Deposit insurance can reassure covered customers, while also reducing their incentive to monitor the bank’s risk-taking.|The scheme’s jurisdiction, institution, account category and coverage limit matter. Not every product sold by a bank is an insured deposit. Supervision and capital requirements help address the incentive problem created by protection.
institutions-investmentbank|Investment banks help issue securities, advise on business combinations and arrange capital-market transactions.|Issuing a security raises money under its terms. A merger or acquisition combines businesses or transfers control. Commercial banks commonly focus on deposits, payments and lending. Large financial groups can provide both kinds of service through different regulated entities.
institutions-underwriting|In firm-commitment underwriting, the underwriter agrees to buy the securities and resell them under the agreement.|It therefore takes specified risk that the offering cannot be distributed as expected. Best-efforts arrangements allocate responsibilities differently. Bookbuilding gathers indications of investor demand. A later price jump does not prove that the correct issue price was obvious beforehand.
institutions-ratings|A credit rating is a repayment-risk opinion that can change or be wrong. It is not a guarantee.|It does not measure every source of market-price or trading-liquidity risk. Being paid by issuers and operating in a concentrated industry can create conflicts for rating providers. Investors still need to understand the claim and the assumptions behind the opinion.
institutions-roles|A broker acts for clients; a dealer trades for its own account.|A market maker supplies quotes at which it is willing to buy or sell. A single firm can perform more than one role. Knowing who is acting for whom helps identify costs, conflicts and responsibility for the trade.
institutions-spread|The bid–ask spread is the asking price minus the offered buying price, usually in dollars per share.|A buyer commonly pays the ask, while a seller receives the bid. Dealers face the risk of holding inventory and of trading with someone who knows more, as well as processing costs. These can help explain why the prices differ.
institutions-orders|A limit order states the maximum purchase price or minimum sale price you will accept.|The limit is normally dollars per share for stock trades. The order may never fill. A market order prioritizes execution at available prices and can receive a worse price than expected in a thin market. Stop orders introduce separate trigger and execution considerations.
institutions-pfof|Payment for routing customer orders can create a conflict between a broker’s revenue and the customer’s execution interests.|A zero advertised commission does not mean all trading costs are zero. Compare the actual price, the bid–ask spread and routing quality. The permitted arrangements and relevant duties differ across jurisdictions.
institutions-hft|Fast automated trading can improve quotes in ordinary conditions while the supplied liquidity can retreat during stress.|High-frequency strategies are not all the same. Their inventory decisions, technology dependencies and reactions to informed traders matter. Speed alone does not guarantee that a quote will remain available in a difficult market.
institutions-funds|An ETF can follow an index or be actively managed. ETF describes its exchange-traded structure.|An exchange-traded fund pools assets and has shares trading during the day. Ordinary open-end mutual funds typically transact at a calculated net asset value under their rules. The investment mandate, holdings and costs must be checked separately from the wrapper.
institutions-nav|Authorized participants can exchange ETF shares and the relevant assets or cash through creation and redemption.|This lets them respond when the share trading price differs from underlying value and normally helps close the gap. Trading costs and difficulty selling underlying assets can limit the process. During stress, an apparently stale asset valuation may also explain part of a price gap.
institutions-fees|Fees leave less money invested, so less remains to earn gains in later years.|An expense ratio is generally a percentage charged per year; other charges can be dollar amounts or transaction costs. Compare total costs, relevant taxes and how closely the fund follows its mandate. A cheap fund can still have concentrated or unsuitable exposures.
institutions-endowment|An endowment may have different access, staff, oversight, cash needs and ability to hold investments that are hard to sell.|An individual cannot copy all those features by copying allocation percentages. Swensen’s broader lessons include diversification, a long horizon, attention to costs and fitting investments to obligations. Selecting skilled managers remains difficult, and alternative assets do not automatically improve returns.
crises-leverage|With assets equal to five times equity, the owner’s own stake is one-fifth of asset value.|That fraction comes from equity ÷ assets = 1 ÷ 5. With debt unchanged, the dollar asset loss falls on the smaller equity stake. Ignoring interest and fees, equity’s percentage return is the asset percentage return multiplied by assets/equity. A large enough decline can use up all equity.
crises-margin|A demand for more collateral can force a sale before an investment has time to recover.|Collateral is cash or assets pledged to support an obligation. A margin call asks for more. Initial margin supports opening a position; variation margin covers changes in its value. Cash can therefore be needed before the final outcome, even when another exposure eventually offsets some losses.
crises-cycle|Falling prices can reduce borrowing capacity and force selling that pushes prices down further.|At the same time, lenders may increase haircuts: they lend a smaller fraction of the collateral’s value. Borrowers then need more of their own money. This feedback can amplify an initial shock even without an equally large new deterioration in the assets’ future payments.
crises-2008|Mortgage losses became more damaging through heavy borrowing, weak loan checks, packaged securities and funding that could disappear quickly.|Many institutions held related housing exposures. When confidence fell, uncertainty about those claims made lending between institutions harder. Lehman Brothers’ failure intensified the stress, but the weaknesses had built up earlier. The crisis had several reinforcing causes.
crises-svb|Low credit risk in securities does not remove interest-rate price losses or a bank’s vulnerability to rapid withdrawals.|Silicon Valley Bank’s long-term asset values fell as rates rose. Its concentrated, largely uninsured deposit base could withdraw quickly. Risk management and supervisory shortcomings also mattered. Owning assets likely to repay eventually is different from having enough cash to meet demands today.
crises-contagion|Distress can spread through shared investments, funding markets and confidence as well as direct loans between firms.|A forced sale can lower the market value of other institutions’ holdings. A common lender may withdraw funding from several borrowers at once. An institution therefore need not have lent directly to the first failing firm to be affected.
crises-shadow|Financial activity outside ordinary banks can still involve heavy borrowing, cash-timing mismatches and connections to the rest of the system.|Funds and other intermediaries provide useful credit. Their rules on withdrawals, collateral and funding can nevertheless create vulnerabilities. Moving an activity to a different legal structure can change where risk accumulates without making it disappear.
crises-resolution|Resolution aims to manage a failing institution, allocate losses and limit disruption to critical services under the law.|Authorities need plans for who takes losses and how essential functions continue. A bail-in imposes specified losses on owners or creditors; a bail-out involves outside support. The available tools and protections depend on the legal framework.
crises-micro_macro|System-wide supervision examines common exposures and feedback that separate firm-by-firm checks may miss.|Microprudential supervision concerns individual institutions. Macroprudential policy asks how their actions interact. If many cautious firms all sell the same assets at once, the combined effect can destabilize the market despite each firm trying to protect itself.
society-debtdynamics|Debt relative to GDP changes with borrowing costs, economic growth, the primary budget gap and other adjustments.|Debt/GDP is a ratio. Its change is measured in percentage points when multiplied by 100. Interest and growth must use consistent periods and price conventions. The exercise’s approximation adds the primary deficit to the interest-minus-growth effect on existing debt; the exact simple ratio also adjusts for GDP growth in the denominator.
society-currency|Issuing the borrowing currency changes a government’s constraints, but does not guarantee investors’ purchasing power.|Inflation can reduce what repaid dollars buy. A currency decline can reduce their value to a foreign investor. Political and institutional constraints still matter. Repaying the promised number of currency units is different from preserving their real value.
society-municipal|General-obligation and revenue bonds rely on different specified repayment sources.|A general-obligation pledge concerns broader issuer backing under the relevant law. A revenue bond relies on named receipts, such as a project’s revenue. Check the legal pledge, available resources and tax treatment. Neither label alone proves the borrowing is safe.
society-social|Public social insurance can share risks and broaden protection where private coverage is difficult or expensive.|Examples include unemployment and retirement protection. A public system can redistribute resources as well as pool uncertainty. It still faces questions about incentives, tax funding, population changes and which benefits it promises.
society-pensions|In a defined-contribution plan, the participant’s resources depend on contributions and investment performance.|Contributions are dollar amounts paid into an account. They do not guarantee a particular future monthly income. A defined-benefit plan instead promises a payment formula, shifting more funding responsibility to the sponsor. Its solvency and legal protections still matter, alongside inflation and lifetime needs.
society-longevity|A lifetime annuity can help manage the risk of living longer than the money set aside for retirement.|People have different lifetimes, allowing the insurer to pool that uncertainty. Payments are dollars per stated period while the covered person lives. A fixed payment does not automatically rise with living costs. Insurer strength, access to invested money and contract terms remain important.
society-nonprofit|A nonprofit restricts profit distribution to private owners; a cooperative is owned and governed by members.|Both can earn a surplus needed for operations. Their rules can support purposes different from maximizing returns to outside shareholders. They still need oversight because managers can have incentives different from those of members or beneficiaries.
society-advice|How an adviser is paid can influence product recommendations and create conflicts.|A fee might be paid by the client or connected with a product sold. Clients need to understand costs, obligations and the adviser’s role. Good advice should fit the person’s capacity for loss, upcoming expenses and time horizon. Legal duties vary by arrangement and jurisdiction.
society-democratization|Useful financial access helps more people fund productive activity and protect important risks.|Access needs understandable terms, suitable protection and attention to costs. Making heavily borrowed speculation easier is not enough to establish an improvement in people’s lives. Ask what decisions and protections the arrangement actually makes possible.
modern-tplus|T+1 means settlement one business day after the trade date for covered transactions.|Settlement completes the transfer of securities and money. It is different from agreeing the trade. The U.S. transition took effect on May 28, 2024, according to the SEC. Relevant weekends and holidays do not count as business days.
modern-capital26|The March 2026 bank-capital package was issued as proposals for public comment, rather than automatically effective final rules.|It addressed Basel components, standardized capital and the surcharge for global systemically important banks. A surcharge is an additional requirement. Read each proposal’s scope and status separately from adopted rules and implementation dates. The app’s dated source snapshot does not refresh automatically.
modern-stress26|The September 2026 stress-test rules were separate final rules with their own implementation timetable.|The Federal Reserve finalized public input on scenarios and material model changes. Averaging stress capital buffer requirements over two years starts in 2028. That buffer is an additional capital requirement linked to modeled stress losses. These changes are distinct from the broader bank-capital proposals.
modern-private|Infrequent valuation can make reported returns look smooth without removing the borrower’s real financial risks.|A loan’s economic value can change between valuation dates. Check the borrower’s debt burden, protections in the loan contract, repayment capacity and need to replace maturing debt. A steady reported price does not guarantee an easy cash exit.
modern-liquidity|Investors may ask for cash sooner than the fund can sell or collect its investments without a large price concession.|That is a liquidity mismatch. Notice periods give advance warning of withdrawals. Gates limit withdrawals, and liquidity buffers provide cash or readily saleable assets. Read the actual redemption terms rather than assuming a fund’s label describes how quickly money can be returned.
modern-stable|A stablecoin depends on its supporting assets, conversion mechanism, redemption rights and the arrangements for holding and controlling those assets.|The peg is a target value, not a guarantee. Reserve-backed and algorithmic designs use different mechanisms and face different weaknesses. Legal protections and rules depend on the structure and jurisdiction. Examine what a holder can actually receive and who must deliver it.
modern-token|Recording a bond as a token does not remove the borrower’s obligation to pay or the possibility of default.|Tokenization changes how a claim may be recorded or transferred. The holder still needs valid legal rights, reliable custody and a workable settlement process. A ledger entry alone does not automatically establish ownership of an asset that can be redeemed.
modern-crypto|Losing or exposing signing keys can mean losing access to assets or allowing someone else to transfer them.|Self-custody leaves key security and recovery with the owner. An exchange or custodian adds dependence on its own operations and obligations. Program errors, governance problems and market manipulation are separate risks; a favorable price history does not prove technical security.
modern-ai|A good historical backtest can fail in live use because it used hindsight, fitted accidental patterns, ignored costs or relied on relationships that changed.|Overfitting means learning sample-specific noise. Data leakage uses information unavailable at the real decision time. Test on genuinely unfamiliar data, include realistic execution and examine adaptation by other participants. A historical return percentage is not a promised future annual rate.
modern-concentration|A capitalization-weighted index can put much of its money into a few large firms even when it contains many names.|A holding’s weight is a percentage of invested money. Count the important exposures across industries, countries and common risk sources. Many small positions do not make large dominant positions disappear or make all returns independent.
modern-climate|Changing hazards and where assets are located can change the size and joint occurrence of insurance losses.|Historical averages may not describe a changing setting. Exposure growth, prevention, insurer resources and reinsurance capacity also affect coverage. Reinsurance is insurance bought by insurers to share some losses; it does not eliminate the underlying catastrophe.
modern-cyber|A shared operational failure can disrupt several otherwise separate financial firms together.|Banks and funds can depend on the same payment system, custodian, cloud service or data provider. Independent portfolios can therefore share one operational weakness. Redundancy, recovery plans and realistic testing help reduce the disruption without eliminating every possible failure.
